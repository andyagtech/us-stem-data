Foreign nationals receive a plurality
of U.S. graduate STEM degrees.
They are mostly federally funded

Across the eight core STEM fields, non-resident students on temporary visas received 40.2% of master's degrees and 38.8% of research doctorates awarded between 2021 and 2025.

In 1979, temporary-visa holders took 11.59% of all U.S. research doctorates. By 2024 that had reached 33.32% — close to a threefold increase in the share.

1. Where the imbalance grew

The master's degree is where the change happened

Between 2008 and 2025, core-STEM master's degrees awarded to U.S. domestic students rose 114%. Awards to non-residents rose 377% — more than three times as fast. Domestic production did not fall; it was outpaced.

Core-STEM master's degrees conferred, 2008–2025. Non-resident awards grew from 27,741 to 132,288; domestic awards from 72,882 to 156,115. Source: IPEDS Completions, CIP families 11, 14, 15, 26, 27, 30, 40, 42.

The two levels behave differently

DoctorateMaster's

Non-resident share of core-STEM degrees conferred, by level, 2008–2025. The master's share rose from 27.6% to 45.9%. The doctorate share was 39.0% in 2008 and 37.5% in 2025.

An important qualification. At the doctorate level the share is not rising. It has moved within a narrow band for nearly two decades and sits below its 2021 peak of 40.1%. Since 2008, domestic doctorate output grew 44% against 35% for non-residents. The doctorate concern is the sustained level — roughly two in five — not a worsening trend.

3. Who the degrees go to

The categories are one field, not two: non-resident sits beside the race groups

IPEDS records every completion in a single field with nine mutually exclusive values. “Non-resident” is one of them, alongside the race categories — not a separate citizenship axis above them. A permanent resident is counted under their race; a foreign national on a student visa is counted as non-resident whatever their race. The data contains no citizen-versus-foreign split, and cannot separate citizens from permanent residents.

4. Who pays

The taxpayer-funded route through a doctorate is used disproportionately by visa holders

The Survey of Earned Doctorates asks every recipient how their doctoral study was primarily funded. Among science and engineering doctorate recipients in 2024, 76.0% of temporary-visa holders were supported principally by a research or teaching assistantship, against 53.1% of U.S. citizens and permanent residents.

The mirror of that gap is self-funding. U.S. citizens were 4.6 times more likely to pay their own way: 16.0% funded themselves from personal or family resources, against 3.5% of visa holders.

5. After the degree

The pipeline does not end at graduation: work authorisation has grown faster than the degrees

6. Around the cap

The capped route has stopped growing; the uncapped one has not

The H-1B is limited by statute to 85,000 new petitions a year. The O-1, for workers of “extraordinary ability”, has no numerical limit at all. Over the five years where both agencies count the same way, approvals moved in opposite directions: H-1B petitions -1%, O-1A petitions +42%.

7. The exemption

The cap does not apply to universities — or to nonprofits affiliated with them

Three employer types are exempt from the H-1B numerical cap by statute: institutions of higher education, nonprofits related to or affiliated with one, and nonprofit or government research organisations. They file without entering the lottery.

Petitions from the affiliated-nonprofit category rose 124% between 2018 and 2025, from 10,734 to 24,073. Universities themselves rose 38%.

15. Limits

What these data do and do not establish

What the data supports

  • Non-resident students receive a plurality of U.S. core-STEM master's degrees (40.2%) and close to two in five research doctorates (38.8%); the temporary-visa share of all U.S. research doctorates rose from 11.59% in 1979 to 33.32% in 2024.
  • Visa-holding doctorate recipients are substantially more likely to be funded through assistantships, and far less likely to self-fund, than citizens.
  • Petitions from nonprofits affiliated with universities, which are exempt from the H-1B cap, rose 124% between FY2018 and FY2025.
  • An uncapped visa category grew while the capped one did not: O-1A petitions +42% against H-1B -1%, 2019 to 2023.
  • Post-graduation work authorisation has grown sharply: STEM OPT went from 2 authorisations in 2007 to 95,384 in 2024.

What the data does not support

  • It does not establish causation. Nothing here shows that any U.S. citizen was denied a place or a funded position because it went to a non-resident. The data records who received degrees and how they were funded, not who applied or was refused.
  • The doctorate share is not rising. It has been broadly flat since 2008 and is below its 2021 peak. Presenting the doctorate as a worsening trend would misstate the record.
  • Assistantship funding is not broken out by ultimate source. The SED records that a recipient was supported by a research assistantship; it does not attribute that support to a specific federal award. The per-doctorate federal figure on page 5 is an intensity ratio, not a cost of production.
  • The visa data on page 7 identifies no employer or industry. It counts petitions and visas nationally by classification. It shows an uncapped category growing while a capped one did not; it cannot show which firms used it, nor that any employer turned to the O-1 because it lost an H-1B. That substitution is an inference from the pattern, not a measurement. Note also that O-1 and L-1 figures are consular visa issuances while O-1A and H-1B are USCIS petition approvals, so each is compared only with its own kind; the window starts at 2019 because USCIS changed its counting basis then and publishes nothing for FY2018.
  • The employer table on page 6 is a Top 200 ranking, not a census. The OPT series beside it is a complete count, but an employer outside the list is absent rather than at zero, so that table cannot be summed to a total.
  • "Non-resident" is a visa status recorded at graduation. It is not ethnicity, not country of origin, and not a statement about any individual's intentions or contribution.

16. Method and sources

How these figures were produced

Method

Degree counts are from IPEDS Completions, restricted to eight core-STEM CIP families (11, 14, 15, 26, 27, 30, 40, 42), using award levels 7 for master's and 17 for research doctorates. Domestic counts are derived as total minus non-resident. Doctorate citizenship figures for all fields come from the NSF Survey of Earned Doctorates, a census rather than a sample. Citizenship detail is unavailable in IPEDS before 1995; 1990 and 1999 are not published by NCES.

17. Data needs

Six things that would settle questions this document cannot

Each item was searched for before being listed. In every case the agency collects the information; none of it is published in a usable form.

1 · Cap status on the petition record — USCIS

Form I-129 records whether a petition is cap-exempt and on what ground. The Report to Congress publishes national totals by category; the Employer Data Hub names every employer but carries no cap field. So the 24,073 affiliated-nonprofit petitions on page 8 cannot be attributed to any organisation. Would settle: which employers use the exemption, at what scale, and whether use is concentrated.

2 · Ultimate source of doctoral assistantship funding — NSF

The Survey of Earned Doctorates records that a recipient was funded by a research assistantship, not which award paid for it. No table in the survey's financial support chapter separates federal from institutional funding. The figure on page 5 is therefore an intensity ratio, not a cost. Would settle: what federal research funding actually spends per doctoral student, by citizenship.

3 · Citizens reported separately from permanent residents

The SED collects three statuses — citizen, permanent resident, temporary visa — but every published table collapses the first two. IPEDS does not collect the distinction at all: a permanent resident is counted under their race. “Domestic” throughout this document therefore means citizens and permanent residents together. Would settle: the citizen share of U.S. graduate STEM degrees, which no published source reports.

4 · OPT by institution, for every institution — DHS

SEVP publishes a Top 100 ranking. An institution outside it is absent rather than zero, so school totals are a floor and movement between years may only reflect the cutoff. Complete school-level data exists but is released to school officials for their own institution, or by FOIA. Would settle: OPT and STEM OPT participation for every institution.

5 · Graduate applications and admissions — NCES

IPEDS counts degrees conferred. Its Admissions component covers first-time undergraduates only, and no federal collection records who applied to graduate programmes, who was admitted, or who was offered funding. Would settle: whether domestic applicants are declining, being declined, or not applying — the question behind this document, and the one it cannot answer.

6 · O-1 and L-1 petitions by employer — USCIS

USCIS publishes employer data hubs for H-1B, H-2A and H-2B, but not for O-1. Issuances are reported by visa classification only. Would settle: whether the growth in the uncapped O-1 category on page 7 is concentrated in the industries most affected by the H-1B cap.
