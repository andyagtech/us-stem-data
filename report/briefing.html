Across the eight core STEM fields, non-resident students on temporary visas received 40.2% of master's degrees and 38.8% of research doctorates awarded between 2021 and 2025.

Summary · one

Five findings, each from a federal source

1 · The shift is at master’s level, and it has not turned

Non-residents took 24.4% of core-STEM master’s degrees in 1995 and 45.9% in 2025. The doctorate moved +4.8 points over the same thirty years, peaked in 2021 and has fallen since. American output rose at both levels — master’s +189% — so this is a change in share, not an American retreat. IPEDS Completions. Page 6.

2 · The federally funded route is the one Americans take least

Research assistantships are how federal money reaches doctoral students: 52,428 federally supported research assistantships against 1,041 teaching ones. They fund 48.9% of visa holders against 29.3% of citizens — a gap of 19.6 points that has not fallen below 19.3 in any of the seven years on record. NSF SED and GSS. Pages 17, 18.

3 · The money and the enrolment are in the same institutions

At 108 institutions a majority of graduate students hold a temporary visa. Those institutions hold $19.4bn of federal research expenditure — 32.9% of the matched total — and 210,870 graduate students. A second model sits beside it: at the highest-share master’s programmes there is almost no federal research money and $3.36bn a year in published tuition billed to non-resident master’s graduates. NSF HERD and GSS; IPEDS. Pages 10, 11.

4 · The work visa is cheaper twice over

First on the wage: 78.7% of the 565,966 H-1B filings that can be priced promise less than the median wage for their occupation in the state where the work is. For software developers the four legal wage levels run 63%, 74%, 91% and 105% of that median — three of the four sit below it. Then on the tax: wages earned on Optional Practical Training carry no Social Security or Medicare, saving the employer 7.65% of payroll across 289,938 authorisations. DOL OFLC and BLS OEWS state file; IRC 3121(b)(19); DHS SEVP. Pages 21, 20.

5 · The student visa is the front of a route to permanent residence

58.3% of those who earned a U.S. doctorate on a temporary visa now hold citizenship or a green card; only 9.8% remain on a temporary visa. Nationally, 74.6% of employment-based green cards go to people already in the country, against 5.4% on the family route. NSF SDR; DHS Yearbook. Pages 12, 13.

Summary · two

What follows from it, and what the data will not support

The mechanism, in one line

Graduate study is the entry point; the work visa is cheaper to the employer than an American at the same salary; and the green card that ends it is filed by that employer, for a named job. An Indian national reaching the front of the EB-2 queue has waited 12.7 years — a category that was current before 2005 — against 1.2 for everyone else. Through all of it, leaving the employer means starting again. State Department Visa Bulletin. Page 23.

Where enforcement is

The Labor Department has found H-1B violations in 2,887 investigations since 2000, agreeing $123m in back wages for 13,640 employees. Measured against the size of the programme, it looks less often than it used to: 2.4 investigations per 10,000 H-1B visas issued in 2024, against 26.2 at the peak. DOL WHISARD. Page 24.

What this document does not claim

  • Not that foreign graduates are paid less. Compared within a graduation cohort, pay is level. That comparison was run, and the first version of it — a $30,000 gap — was withdrawn once cohort was controlled.
  • Not that federal grants paid named foreign students. Research expenditure is recorded at the institution and never at a person. The claim is about institutions and their graduate operations in bulk.
  • Not that violations rose or fell. Enforcement counts what was found, which needs a complaint. A falling line is equally consistent with fewer complaints from workers who depend on the employer they would report.
  • Not that Americans left STEM. Domestic output rose at every level measured. What fell is the domestic share, and the rate at which American bachelor’s graduates go on to a doctorate.

The three things no federal source will answer

Cap-exempt status per employer, which USCIS records and does not publish. Graduate applications and admissions, which no agency collects — so falling domestic conversion cannot be attributed to choice, rejection or funding. And citizens separated from permanent residents at conferral, collected in three categories and published in two. Each is set out on page 38.

1. Where the imbalance grew

The master's degree is where the change happened

Between 2008 and 2025, core-STEM master's degrees awarded to U.S. domestic students rose 114%. Awards to non-residents rose 377% — more than three times as fast. Domestic production did not fall; it was outpaced.

The master’s is the level that pays. Almost none of it is funded, and the billings have quadrupled

9. From non-immigrant to immigrant

The student visa is not a temporary category. For most who finish a doctorate on one, it is the first step to permanent residence

Every count on page 6 is a visa count, which invites the assumption that these are visitors. The Survey of Doctorate Recipients follows the same people afterwards. Of those who earned a U.S. doctorate while on a temporary visa, 58.3% now hold citizenship or permanent residence — and only 9.8% are still on a temporary visa.

Read the two pages together. A non-resident share of 44.8% of graduate STEM degrees does not measure temporary visitors passing through. It measures the entry point of a route to settlement, and 86% of those who stay complete it.

The survey covers every cohort from the 1970s on, so the balance between staying and leaving describes the whole period rather than any single year. Recent graduates have had less time to naturalise, which if anything understates the eventual rate. Status is recorded at the doctorate and again at survey; nothing here identifies which visa or which route was used, and the SED does not follow master’s recipients at all — so the transition is measured at doctorate level and applied to the larger population only by inference.

14. Who pays

The taxpayer-funded route through a doctorate is used disproportionately by visa holders

The Survey of Earned Doctorates asks every recipient how their doctoral study was primarily funded. Among science and engineering doctorate recipients in 2024, 76.0% of temporary-visa holders were supported principally by a research or teaching assistantship, against 53.1% of U.S. citizens and permanent residents.

The mirror of that gap is self-funding. U.S. citizens were 4.6 times more likely to pay their own way: 16.0% funded themselves from personal or family resources, against 3.5% of visa holders.

17. The discount

Equal pay is not equal cost: an American graduate is the more expensive hire at the same salary

Compared within a graduation cohort, doctorate holders trained on a temporary visa earn about what their American classmates earn: among those who finished in 2015 or later, a median of $103,000 against $100,000 for citizens trained here. That is a statement about salary, and salary is not what an employer pays. Wages earned on Optional Practical Training are exempt from Social Security and Medicare tax under IRC 3121(b)(19). The graduate is cheaper to employ at the same salary.

18. What was promised

Two in three H-1B filings name the bottom of the legal wage range

Every H-1B filing states a prevailing-wage level for the occupation, from I to IV. Level I is the entry rate, Level IV the experienced one. Across 1,150,107 certified filings in 2019 and 2023, 64.6% named Level I or Level II.

Certified H-1B filings by prevailing-wage level. Source: DOL Office of Foreign Labor Certification disclosure data.

Among the largest filers the concentration is higher still: Tata Consultancy files 86.2% of its certified applications at Level I or II and Wipro 90.0%, across 2,973 employers filing at least 50 times.

What those levels pay, against the occupation

The levels are positions in a distribution. Setting the wage promised beside what the occupation actually pays gives the size of the difference — and the yardstick matters. Against the national median for each occupation, 70.8% of filings promise less. Against the median in the state where the work is, 78.7% do. Filings concentrate in high-wage states, so the national comparison flatters them: in California 38% of filings fall below the national median for their occupation, but 70% fall below the Californian one.

20. Who holds the leverage

The employer holds the visa. Losing the job means losing the right to remain in the country, and the worker knows it

The payroll-tax gap on page 20 is a price difference. This is a bargaining difference, and it is larger. H-1B status is tied to one employer: the employer files the petition, and on termination the worker has 60 days to find another sponsor or leave the country (8 CFR 214.1(l)(2)). Permanent residence is filed by the employer too, for a named job. An American in the next chair can resign on two weeks’ notice and lose nothing but the job.

The queue is what turns a rule into a decade. India’s EB-2 category was current — no wait at all — through 2004. It passed three years by 2007, five by 2013 and ten by 2016, and stood at 12.7 years in 2023. Every year of that is a year in which leaving the employer means starting again.

22. Around the cap

The capped route has stopped growing; the uncapped one has not

The H-1B is limited by statute to 85,000 new petitions a year. The O-1, for workers of “extraordinary ability”, has no numerical limit at all. Over the five years where both agencies count the same way, approvals moved in opposite directions: H-1B petitions -1%, O-1A petitions +42%.

23. The exemption

The cap does not apply to universities — or to nonprofits affiliated with them

Three employer types are exempt from the H-1B numerical cap by statute: institutions of higher education, nonprofits related to or affiliated with one, and nonprofit or government research organisations. They file without entering the lottery.

Petitions from the affiliated-nonprofit category rose 124% between 2018 and 2025, from 10,734 to 24,073. Universities themselves rose 38%.

32. Limits

What these data do and do not establish

What the data supports

  • Non-resident students receive a plurality of U.S. core-STEM master's degrees (40.2%) and close to two in five research doctorates (38.8%); the temporary-visa share of all U.S. research doctorates rose from 11.59% in 1979 to 33.32% in 2024.
  • Visa-holding doctorate recipients are substantially more likely to be funded through assistantships, and far less likely to self-fund, than citizens.
  • Petitions from nonprofits affiliated with universities, which are exempt from the H-1B cap, rose 124% between FY2018 and FY2025.
  • An uncapped visa category grew while the capped one did not: O-1A petitions +42% against H-1B -1%, 2019 to 2023.
  • Post-graduation work authorisation has grown sharply: STEM OPT went from 2 authorisations in 2007 to 95,384 in 2024.

What the data does not support

  • It does not establish causation. Nothing here shows that any U.S. citizen was denied a place or a funded position because it went to a non-resident. The data records who received degrees and how they were funded, not who applied or was refused.
  • The doctorate share is not rising. It has been broadly flat since 2008 and is below its 2021 peak. Presenting the doctorate as a worsening trend would misstate the record.
  • Assistantship funding is not broken out by ultimate source. The SED records that a recipient was supported by a research assistantship; it does not attribute that support to a specific federal award. The per-doctorate federal figure on page 17 is an intensity ratio, not a cost of production.
  • The visa data on page 25 identifies no employer or industry. It counts petitions and visas nationally by classification. It shows an uncapped category growing while a capped one did not; it cannot show which firms used it, nor that any employer turned to the O-1 because it lost an H-1B. That substitution is an inference from the pattern, not a measurement. Note also that O-1 and L-1 figures are consular visa issuances while O-1A and H-1B are USCIS petition approvals, so each is compared only with its own kind; the window starts at 2019 because USCIS changed its counting basis then and publishes nothing for FY2018.
  • The employer table on page 19 is a Top 200 ranking, not a census. The OPT series beside it is a complete count, but an employer outside the list is absent rather than at zero, so that table cannot be summed to a total.
  • "Non-resident" is a visa status recorded at graduation. It is not ethnicity, not country of origin, and not a statement about any individual's intentions or contribution.

33. Method and sources

How these figures were produced

Method

Degree counts are from IPEDS Completions, restricted to eight core-STEM CIP families (11, 14, 15, 26, 27, 30, 40, 42), using award levels 7 for master's and 17 for research doctorates. Domestic counts are derived as total minus non-resident. Doctorate citizenship figures for all fields come from the NSF Survey of Earned Doctorates, a census rather than a sample. Citizenship detail is unavailable in IPEDS before 1995; 1990 and 1999 are not published by NCES.

34. Data needs

Seven things that would settle questions this document cannot

Each item was searched for before being listed. In every case the agency collects the information; none of it is published in a usable form. The four below concern how the pipeline is measured; the three on the next page concern who is using it.

1 · Cap status on the petition record — USCIS

Form I-129 records whether a petition is cap-exempt and on what ground. The Report to Congress publishes national totals by category; the Employer Data Hub names every employer but carries no cap field. So the 24,073 affiliated-nonprofit petitions on page 26 cannot be attributed to any organisation. Would settle: which employers use the exemption, at what scale, and whether use is concentrated.

2 · Federal funding crossed with citizenship — NSF

The mechanism half is answered: federal money reaches doctoral students overwhelmingly through research assistantships, 52,428 against 1,041 teaching ones, which is what licenses pages 17 and 18. The crossing is not. GSS reports support by agency and enrolment by citizenship, never together; the SED records the assistantship but not which award paid. No published field says which agency funded a foreign national. Would settle: what federal research funding spends per doctoral student, by citizenship — turning a seven-year proxy into a measurement.

3 · Citizens separated from permanent residents at conferral

Available after graduation — the Survey of Doctorate Recipients records four categories, native citizen, naturalised, permanent resident, temporary visa — but not at conferral. Every published SED table collapses the first two, and IPEDS does not collect the distinction at all: a permanent resident is counted under their race. “Domestic” in the degree counts throughout this document therefore means citizens and permanent residents together. Would settle: the citizen share of U.S. graduate STEM degrees, which no published source reports.

4 · OPT by institution, for every institution — DHS

SEVP publishes a Top 100 ranking. An institution outside it is absent rather than zero, so school totals are a floor and movement between years may only reflect the cutoff. Complete school-level data exists but is released to school officials for their own institution, or by FOIA. Would settle: OPT and STEM OPT participation for every institution.

34. Data needs, continued

Who is using the pipeline — and one ask that needs no new collection at all

5 · Graduate applications and admissions — NCES

IPEDS counts degrees conferred. Its Admissions component covers first-time undergraduates only, and no federal collection records who applied to graduate programmes, who was admitted, or who was offered funding. Would settle: whether domestic applicants are declining, being declined, or not applying — the question behind this document, and the one it cannot answer.

6 · L-1 and O-1 petitions by employer — USCIS

For H-1B this document can name the employer three times over: what it requested, what USCIS granted, what the Labor Department found. For L-1 and O-1 it cannot name the employer once. Neither classification requires a labour condition application, so the Labor Department never sees them, and USCIS publishes an employer data hub for H-1B, H-2A and H-2B but not for these. The closest substitute is the destination state declared on arrival — L-1 concentrates in Texas, California, New York and Michigan — which is a location, not an employer.

The specific ask. USCIS already holds this: the petitioner name and address on Form I-129 for classifications L-1A, L-1B, O-1A and O-1B, aggregated by petitioner and fiscal year with approvals and denials. That is the same field, from the same form and the same system, that produces the published H-1B Employer Data Hub — the extract exists for one classification and not the others. No new collection is required. Would settle: which companies transfer the most staff into the United States without a wage test, and whether O-1 growth sits in the industries most constrained by the H-1B cap.

7 · Student visas by institution — State and DHS

The State Department reports visa issuance by consulate and nationality and never by school; SEVP publishes school-level figures for OPT alone. So the institutions that bring in the most foreign students cannot be identified directly. What can be shown is who enrols them, from IPEDS, and who places the most into STEM OPT afterwards — two different populations, neither of which is an F-1 count. Would settle: where the student pipeline actually enters, which is the first stage of everything else in this document.
