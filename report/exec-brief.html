Executive brief · Built from federal data · October 2026

American universities, paid for by American taxpayers,
now award most graduate seats in a growing number of
programs to people who are not Americans

Across the eight core STEM fields, non-resident students on temporary visas received 40.2% of master's degrees awarded between 2021 and 2025 — 475,551 of 1,182,438. In 2024 they took 33.3% of all US research doctorates.

Those are averages, and the average hides the finding. At 121 of the 567 institutions large enough to measure, most of the master's degrees awarded go to people who are not Americans. At 56 institutions the same is true of the doctorate.

A graduate seat is finite. An American who is not admitted does not receive a smaller share of a program; they receive none of it. Where most of a publicly funded program's seats go to temporary visa holders, year after year, the effect on American applicants is the same whatever anyone intended — and the people funding it are the ones going without.

The public pays

Federal and state money funds the result, and cannot fund the people it was raised for

Federal research funding runs to roughly $875,000 for every STEM research doctorate awarded. State legislatures fund the public institutions on top of that, and founded them to educate the people of those states.

57 institutions have graduate enrollments that are mostly foreign; at several, nine in ten. 53 of them take federal student aid, drawing $388m in Pell grants and $618m in federal loans. Foreign students cannot receive either. That money is American students' aid, paid to institutions whose graduate programs are mostly not for Americans.

The public payroll sponsors directly as well. Universities, school districts, public utilities and agencies of state government filed 42,833 certified applications for permanent residence across FY2013-FY2024 — 32,121 by universities, 7,804 by school districts, 2,821 by utilities and 87 by state agencies. Each one required the employer to test the local labor market first and conclude that no American would do.

Sources: NSF NCSES Federal Support Survey; IPEDS Fall Enrollment and Student Financial Aid; US Department of Labor PERM disclosure files. Every figure is computed from those files when this document is built.

What the degree is for

The degree has become a route to a work visa, and the worker it produces is cheaper than the American beside him

The graduate degree is the product and the work visa is why it sells. On the far side of it, 1,106 firms whose business is supplying workers to other companies filed 252,806 certified H-1B applications — 12.2% of every certified application in those years. That classification comes from the employers' own answers on their own forms, made under penalty of perjury.

The wage data says what the arrangement is worth. Of 565,966 certified applications priced against the local market, 78.7% were below the median wage for that occupation in that place. A worker whose right to remain is tied to an employer does not negotiate, and the discount is the point.

An American graduate competing for these jobs is not competing on merit against an equal. They are competing against a cheaper worker who cannot leave, supplied at volume by firms built for the purpose — after competing for the graduate seat that produced that worker, at an institution their own taxes fund.

What follows

What the record supports, and what it does not

What the data establishes. That public money funds these programs; that a large and growing number of them award most of their seats to temporary visa holders; that institutions with mostly foreign graduate enrollments still draw federal student aid Americans alone can use; and that the workers these programs produce are paid below the local market more often than not.

What it does not establish. That any institution or firm broke a law. Nothing in these records shows that, and this brief does not say it. The records show what institutions chose, repeatedly, with public money, and that is a question of policy rather than of legality.

What cannot be answered at all. No federal collection records who applied to a graduate program, who was admitted, or who was offered funding. The question of whether American applicants are declining, being declined, or not applying is the central one, and no agency gathers the data to answer it. That gap, and twenty-three others, are set out at phd-demographics.andy-barr.com/data_requests — most needing no new collection at all.
