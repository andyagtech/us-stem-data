13. Data needs

Six things that would settle questions this document cannot

Each item was searched for before being listed. In every case the agency collects the information; none of it is published in a usable form. Two items are narrower than they were: a second survey has since answered part of each, and only the remainder is stated here.

1 · Cap status on the petition record — USCIS

Form I-129 records whether a petition is cap-exempt and on what ground. The Report to Congress publishes national totals by category; the Employer Data Hub names every employer but carries no cap field. So the 24,073 affiliated-nonprofit petitions on page {{page:exemption}} cannot be attributed to any organisation. Would settle: which employers use the exemption, at what scale, and whether use is concentrated.

2 · Federal funding crossed with citizenship — NSF

This was listed as the source of assistantship funding being unknown. The Survey of Graduate Students and Postdoctorates has since answered the mechanism question: federal money reaches doctoral students overwhelmingly through research assistantships — 52,428 federally supported research assistantships against 1,041 federally supported teaching assistantships — which is what licenses the reading of pages {{page:whopays}} and {{page:supporttrend}}. What remains is the crossing. GSS reports support by agency, and reports enrolment by citizenship, but never the two together; the SED records the assistantship but not which award paid for it. No published field says which agency funded a foreign national. Would settle: what federal research funding spends per doctoral student, by citizenship — turning a seven-year proxy into a measurement.

3 · Citizens separated from permanent residents at conferral

This was listed as unavailable anywhere. It is available after graduation: the Survey of Doctorate Recipients records status at the doctorate in four categories — native citizen, naturalised citizen, permanent resident, temporary visa. It is not available at conferral. Every published SED table collapses citizens and permanent residents, and IPEDS does not collect the distinction at all: a permanent resident is counted under their race. “Domestic” in the degree counts throughout this document therefore means citizens and permanent residents together. Would settle: the citizen share of U.S. graduate STEM degrees, which no published source reports.

4 · OPT by institution, for every institution — DHS

SEVP publishes a Top 100 ranking. An institution outside it is absent rather than zero, so school totals are a floor and movement between years may only reflect the cutoff. Complete school-level data exists but is released to school officials for their own institution, or by FOIA. Would settle: OPT and STEM OPT participation for every institution.

5 · Graduate applications and admissions — NCES

IPEDS counts degrees conferred. Its Admissions component covers first-time undergraduates only, and no federal collection records who applied to graduate programmes, who was admitted, or who was offered funding. Would settle: whether domestic applicants are declining, being declined, or not applying — the question behind this document, and the one it cannot answer.

6 · O-1 and L-1 petitions by employer — USCIS

USCIS publishes employer data hubs for H-1B, H-2A and H-2B, but not for O-1. Issuances are reported by visa classification only. Would settle: whether the growth in the uncapped O-1 category on page {{page:cap}} is concentrated in the industries most affected by the H-1B cap.
